How to make a hard decision when you keep going in circles

Some decisions get made in thirty seconds. Others take up residence in your head for weeks, sometimes months, like background noise you can't quite silence.

Leave or stay. Change or continue. Say yes to something risky, or say no to something comfortable.

What we don't always understand is why certain decisions resist so much. It isn't a question of intelligence, or a lack of information. We've often been all the way around it. Listed the pros and cons. Asked friends. Gone to sleep thinking we'd know by morning.

And in the morning, still nothing.

What science says about indecision

Persistent indecision is rarely a lack of data. It's almost always a conflict between two systems that don't speak the same language.

On one side, the prefrontal cortex — the brain's analytical part — which evaluates, compares, projects. On the other, the limbic system and the body as a whole — which register an older, more diffuse, but no less real, kind of knowing.

Antonio Damasio showed this as early as 1994, in his research on patients with lesions to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex: stripped of their emotions, these patients became, paradoxically, unable to decide. Not because they lacked reasoning, but because they could no longer feel which option was right. The somatic markers — those subtle bodily signals that steer our choices before we even put them into words — no longer worked.

In other words: deciding well requires listening to the body, not just the mind.

Why going in circles doesn't solve anything

There's a very common illusion: that thinking for long enough will eventually produce the answer.

Sometimes that's true. But when you're going in circles — running through the same arguments in the same order, arriving at the same provisional conclusions, then heading back the other way — that's no longer reflection. It's rumination.

Rumination activates the brain's default mode network — the one that switches on when you're doing nothing, and that produces a kind of circular thinking, oriented toward risk and negative scenarios. Brain-imaging studies (Nolen-Hoeksema, 2008) show that it sustains and amplifies stress without producing any new information.

In other words: the more you ruminate, the less you move forward. And the more exhausted you are by the time you do decide.

What actually helps — and what we don't do enough of

It isn't about thinking less. It's about thinking differently.

Leave your head and go into your body. Not metaphorically. Concretely. Walk, breathe, do something with your hands. Physical movement changes your neurological state and lets your nervous system break out of the rumination loop. This isn't procrastination — it's a necessary condition for clarity to emerge.

Set the decision down instead of analyzing it. Instead of which option is best?, try: if I already knew I'd chosen option A, what would I feel in my body? Expansion or contraction? Lightness or heaviness? The somatic answer is often faster, and more reliable, than the rational one.

Name what you're actually protecting. Hard decisions almost always involve a loss — whichever option you choose. Naming what you stand to lose, on each side, helps clear the fog. It isn't the decision itself that's frightening. It's what it makes impossible.

The role of an introspection tool

There's an ancient practice, found across many traditions, of putting a question to something outside yourself — an image, a text, a draw — not to get a magic answer, but to provoke an inner reaction.

The point isn't that the tool knows. The point is that your reaction to what the tool offers reveals something you already know but haven't yet put into words.

When a randomly drawn card describes exactly what you didn't dare name, that isn't magic. It's projection — a well-documented psychological mechanism — kicking in. You attribute to the symbol what you were already carrying.

Used in this spirit, an oracle isn't an answer. It's a way of bringing things to light.

Three questions to sit with before deciding

If you're facing a decision that resists, here are three questions to hold — not to solve.

What do I lose if I choose each option? Not what I gain — that, we usually know. What we lose.

If I removed fear from the equation — fear of judgment, fear of failure, fear of disappointing — what would remain?

Am I looking for an answer, or am I looking for permission?

This last question is often the most useful. We frequently already know what we want to do. What we're looking for is permission to want it.
